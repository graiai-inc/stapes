## Discussion

Our benchmark demonstrates that open-source, on-device ASR models achieve clinical term accuracy within 1 to 3 percentage points of commercial cloud APIs across three clinical conversation datasets. This performance parity, combined with the elimination of patient audio transmission and recurring API costs, supports the feasibility of privacy-preserving on-device ASR as a foundation for clinical documentation systems.

### Model Selection and Clinical Term Accuracy

No single on-device model dominated across all datasets and metrics. Whisper-turbo achieved the highest clinical term accuracy on two of three datasets, while parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2 achieved the lowest WER on two of three datasets. This divergence suggests that overall transcription accuracy and clinical term accuracy are partially independent, and that model selection for clinical applications should consider domain-specific metrics rather than WER alone. For clinical deployment, a decision framework should account for the conversation type (structured vs. naturalistic), the specialty (our data show performance varies across primary care, psychiatry, and structured examinations), privacy requirements (particularly for sensitive domains such as substance abuse treatment or mental health), cost constraints, and connectivity limitations in rural or resource-limited settings.

### Clinical Safety Implications

Drug names exhibited the highest error rates across all models, with wellbutrin misrecognized in 60.4% of occurrences on the psychiatric dataset, and similarly high rates for ramipril, rosuvastatin, amoxicillin, and lisinopril on primary care and OSCE data. Prior work has found that 5.7 to 8.9 percent of ASR errors in clinical documents are clinically significant, with medication errors carrying the highest risk of patient harm. Our findings suggest that current ASR systems, whether on-device or cloud-based, cannot be relied upon for accurate medication transcription without downstream verification. Any clinical deployment should incorporate safeguards such as medication name verification against formulary databases, clinician review of high-risk terms before finalization, and clear documentation that the transcript is machine-generated. The consistency of these failures across deployment modes indicates that the challenge is intrinsic to current ASR technology rather than specific to on-device inference.

### ROVER Fusion

ROVER hypothesis fusion exhibited strikingly dataset-dependent behavior. On the OSCE dataset, fusion improved WER for every file tested, reducing the best single-model WER by nearly 7 percentage points and outperforming all cloud APIs. On PriMock57 and the psychiatric dataset, fusion degraded performance for every model pair and every file, with no exceptions across 91 pairs and 128 files. Examination of the transcripts revealed that on the OSCE dataset, models produced structurally similar outputs with independent per-word errors amenable to voting-based correction. On the other two datasets, models diverged structurally, and the word-level alignment introduced errors rather than correcting them. This finding has implications for any clinical AI system that relies on multi-model fusion: the benefit of hypothesis combination cannot be assumed and must be validated on representative data for each deployment context. Confidence-weighted fusion methods that use per-token model uncertainty to modulate voting may address this limitation, but this remains to be demonstrated.

### Privacy and Regulatory Considerations

On-device ASR eliminates the transmission of patient audio to third-party servers, addressing one significant component of the privacy concerns associated with cloud-based clinical AI. However, privacy in clinical speech-to-text systems involves broader considerations including patient consent, transparency about ASR use, data governance for locally stored recordings, and accountability for errors. Clinical deployment of ASR systems may also fall under FDA oversight depending on the intended use and risk classification, and responsible deployment requires ongoing performance monitoring, version control, and bias surveillance across patient populations. These governance requirements apply to both on-device and cloud architectures and should be addressed in institutional implementation planning.

### Limitations

This study has several technical limitations. All three datasets consist of simulated or enacted clinical encounters rather than real patient-clinician conversations, which are known to present additional challenges including overlapping speech, background noise, non-lexical conversational sounds, and greater acoustic variability. No open-license datasets of real clinical conversations currently exist, a systemic gap in medical AI research that limits the ecological validity of all ASR benchmarks in this domain. On-device models were evaluated using a single inference engine (sherpa-onnx), and performance may vary under alternative runtimes. We did not assess real-time performance, which is relevant for ambient scribes that must process audio continuously.

Our methodology also has limitations. The UMLS-based clinical term identification, while more comprehensive than pattern matching, includes a manually curated stopword list to address collisions between common English words and UMLS abbreviations or eponyms. This filtering may miss some collisions or exclude valid terms. The multi-word span scoring approach, in which any word error within a medical concept span counts as a span error, may overestimate error rates for longer phrases. One cloud API (Google medical_conversation) exhibited substantially higher WER than other services across all datasets; examination of its transcripts revealed verbatim transcription of filled pauses and disfluencies, illustrating that WER penalizes faithful transcription of speech phenomena equally with genuine recognition errors. Cloud API results and pricing reflect early 2026 and may change; on-device cost comparisons do not account for hardware, IT support, or clinician time spent reviewing and correcting errors.

### Future Directions

Future work should evaluate on real clinical recordings as they become available, such as the Bridge2AI-Voice corpus. The dataset-dependent failure of ROVER fusion motivates investigation of confidence-weighted fusion methods using per-token model probabilities. Specialty-specific benchmarks and evaluation of complete ambient documentation pipelines, from speech recognition through note generation, would provide more clinically meaningful assessments.

## Conclusion

On-device ASR models approach the clinical term accuracy of commercial cloud APIs while eliminating patient audio transmission and recurring costs. The gap is smallest on psychiatric consultations (1 percentage point) and widest on primary care conversations (3 percentage points). ROVER fusion can close or eliminate this gap on structured clinical examinations but degrades performance on naturalistic conversation, a finding not previously reported with implications for any multi-model clinical AI system. These results support the feasibility of privacy-preserving, on-device clinical speech recognition, while highlighting that medication transcription accuracy remains a shared challenge across all current ASR systems and that standardized benchmarks on real clinical data are urgently needed.
