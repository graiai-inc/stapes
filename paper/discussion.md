## Discussion

Our benchmark demonstrates that open-source, on-device ASR models achieve medical term recall within 1 to 3 percentage points of commercial cloud APIs across three clinical conversation datasets. This performance parity, combined with the elimination of patient data transmission and recurring API costs, suggests that on-device ASR is a viable foundation for privacy-preserving clinical documentation systems.

Several findings warrant further discussion. First, no single on-device model dominated across all datasets and metrics. Whisper-turbo achieved the highest medical term recall on two of three datasets, while parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2 achieved the lowest WER on two of three datasets. This divergence between WER and medical term recall suggests that overall transcription accuracy and clinical term accuracy are partially independent, and that model selection for clinical applications should consider domain-specific metrics rather than WER alone.

Second, ROVER hypothesis fusion exhibited strikingly dataset-dependent behavior. On the OSCE dataset, fusion improved WER for every file tested, reducing the best single-model WER by nearly 7 percentage points and outperforming all cloud APIs. On PriMock57 and the psychiatric dataset, fusion degraded performance for every model pair and every file, with no exceptions across 91 pairs and 128 files. Examination of the transcripts revealed that on the OSCE dataset, models produced structurally similar outputs with independent per-word errors amenable to voting-based correction. On the other two datasets, models diverged structurally, and the word-level alignment introduced errors rather than correcting them. This finding has implications for clinical systems that rely on multi-model fusion: the benefit of ROVER cannot be assumed across recording conditions or conversation types.

Third, Google's medical_conversation model consistently exhibited the highest WER among cloud APIs, yet examination of its transcripts revealed that this was partly attributable to verbatim transcription of filled pauses and disfluencies that other services and the reference transcripts omit. This illustrates a known limitation of WER: it penalizes faithful transcription of speech disfluencies equally with genuine recognition errors.

This study has several limitations. All three datasets consist of simulated or enacted clinical encounters rather than real patient-clinician conversations. No open-license datasets of real clinical conversations currently exist, which itself reflects a systemic gap in medical AI research. Performance on real clinical audio, with its greater acoustic variability, overlapping speech, and environmental noise, may differ from our results. On-device models were evaluated using a single inference engine (sherpa-onnx), and performance may vary under alternative runtimes. The UMLS-based medical term identification, while more comprehensive than pattern-based approaches, may still miss domain-specific jargon or produce false positives despite our stopword filtering. Finally, cloud API results reflect pricing and model versions as of early 2026 and may change over time.

Future work should evaluate these models on real clinical recordings as they become available under appropriate data use agreements, such as the Bridge2AI-Voice corpus. The dataset-dependent failure of ROVER fusion motivates investigation of confidence-weighted fusion methods that could potentially recover the benefit observed on structured speech while avoiding degradation on naturalistic conversation.

## Conclusion

On-device ASR models approach the medical term accuracy of commercial cloud APIs while eliminating patient data transmission and recurring costs. The gap is smallest on psychiatric consultations (1 percentage point) and widest on primary care conversations (3 percentage points). ROVER fusion can close or eliminate this gap on structured clinical examinations but degrades performance on naturalistic conversation, a finding not previously reported. These results support the feasibility of privacy-preserving, on-device clinical speech recognition, while highlighting the need for standardized benchmarks on real clinical conversation data.
